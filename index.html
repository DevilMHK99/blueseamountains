Les Racines

The roots — Damascus, and how the family scattered from it

“Khodr begat Henri. Henri begat Elie. Elie begat Michael-Henri. Michael-Henri begat Malakai.”

I

Roots — Damascus

Yvonne and Henri were both Syrian Jews. Their fathers and mothers were Syrian Jews as well. In particular they all were Damascus Jews. Within Syria there were large communities of Jews in Damascus and Aleppo — between 80,000 and 120,000 at the beginning of the 20th century.

Elie

The two Damascus and Aleppo Jewish communities were quite different, mirroring the difference and rivalry between the two cities. The accents in their speeches were quite different. Aleppo had a reputation for a very sharp business and commercial flair.

Elie

The palaces

In August, Elie took the group on a tour of what the community once built: the Farhi Palace of Damascus — “Jews with palaces?!” — and the Bonfils photographs of the Stambouli House courtyard. Over one Farhi door, a Hebrew inscription: “May you be blessed on arrival and blessed be you departing. Through this door no misfortune will happen — neither to old man, nor young child, nor young man, nor anybody else.” And a thought took hold: “Shall we form a group to go to Damascus one day???” “Mmmm… I was there and I loved it,” said Gina.

Henri’s parents were Khodr Krayem and Esther Harari — Henri called his mother “Teira”, the bird. Khodr’s house stood in the Haret Al Yahud, the Jewish quarter of Damascus. The name Khodr is the equivalent of Elie — which is why Elie carries it.

Henri, G'D bless his soul, considered himself an assimilated Jew and we did not live in the Hara, neither in Damascus nor for that purpose in Beirut. We lived in an apartment in a suburb of Damascus for the first five years of my life. I remember vividly the balcony overlooking fields — there were wild dogs barking at night and I used to throw stones at them, collected from the fields earlier in the day under guidance from Henri.

Elie

My other vivid memory of Damascus was a barber shop in the main square, Sahet al Marje. Its entrance was made of a curtain of multicolored beads that rustled every time someone pushed through.

Elie

Yvonne & Henri

Yvonne in Damascus won a prize in stenography because of her advanced French she picked up in Alger. She got a job as a secretary at the Banque de Syrie — presumably this is where she met Henri. Henri could not get married because he was supporting his parents, bankrupted by the 1929 world crisis.

Elie

Ils se sont connus elle 16, lui 26 — et ont attendu 10 ans avant de se marier.

Norma

On weekends they went to the Ghouta — the lush green oasis crossed by streams that was Damascus’s countryside. “So this is where those beautiful pictures were taken, with trees and roaring streams and cuddled baby goats. What a place for love.”

Henri nearly drowned. Yvonne good swimmer. She saved him.

Elie — on a swim at the Tel Aviv beach, 1946

A very risky, very beautiful experience: we were surrounded by the moukhabarat, for protection. Many Jewish families still lived there and seemed free and happy. Beit Liniado — the house on the map

Les Liniado viennent de Barcelone, où ils étaient pêcheurs. Ils ont quitté quand Isabelle la Catholique a jeté les juifs d’Espagne, et sont arrivés à Istanbul… La maison de Damas appartenait aux Stambouli, qui l’ont vendue aux Liniado vers la fin du XIXe siècle — mon grand-père Tewfik Liniado était marié à Adèle Stambouli. Elle existe encore : ma cousine Denise Dweck a été la visiter. Elle était voisine de la maison des Lisbona et de la maison Farhi. À Damas on nous appelait « Niyyado » — on ne prononçait pas le « li ».

Dani Liniado

Then she opened “le livre sublime” — Damascus: Hidden Treasures of the Old City, by Brigid Keenan, the English ambassador’s wife — and there it all was, in print: the map of Harat al-Yahud with Bait Niyadu (Bait Stambuli) marked beside the Great Synagogue, Bait Lisbona and Bait Farhi. The mispronunciation itself, immortalised by cartography.

Finalement, notre cœur appartient au Liban — mais ceux de nos parents, à la Syrie.

Elie Bazbaz
II

Chafica

Chafica was a beautiful woman. She had wide green-mauve eyes and many of us across generations got her eyes or colours close to her eyes: my mum, Norma, Elie, Simone, Tania, MH, Benjamin — and we are all reconnaissants towards her. I can see her after her shower splashing herself all over with eau de cologne 4711 Jean Marie Farina — the cologne I use to this very day.

Elie

Norma says Chafica with her Bazbaz husband went on a ship to Latin America, emigrating as many people from Syria, Jewish or non-Jewish, did at the time. On their way they stopped in Algeria where her two Zagha brothers were established as antiquarians. The Zaghas asked them to stay in Algiers — and that is why Yvonne grew up in Alger, speaking French.

Elie

Family legend adds a twist: after some years Chafica “got involved with a lover in Syria”, packed up her Bazbaz husband and sailed back to Damascus with Yvonne and Lucie — leaving the two boys in Alger. Elie’s verdict, seventy years on: “there is something in the English legal system called the Statute of Limitations, which says that crimes after a long number of years are no longer considered crimes.”

I have a vivid memory of Chatila Zagha, your grandmother. I loved her « awful » reputation (according to my mother), her strong personality, her big mouth and sex-appeal.

Berty Turkieh

La grand-mère Chafika était belle et sympa — elle rigolait avec mon père Joseph Mouadeb et Edmond Safra. Que de souvenirs. France Moreau était dans ma classe — elle était belle et avait beaucoup de succès chez les garçons. Norma Krayem était encore plus belle, mais était plus sage… et douce.

Andrée Mouadeb Sasson — from Montreal, September

France

Bien sûr, j’étais amoureux de ma cousine France.” “Une vie et une passion de la vie” (Elie Bazbaz). “G’d bless her soul.”

Emma Bazbaz

Mon cousin côté maternel habite à Juan-les-Pins. Lorsque je lui ai donné le nom de jeune fille de Teta, il a fait le rapprochement avec l’inscription sur la synagogue — le nom de David Zagha, qui l’a fondée. Je lui ai dit : mais c’est mon grand-oncle !!

Les contes de tante Chafika

In September Gaby Elia began writing the Chafica stories down properly — three tales so far, each “basée sur des faits véridiques.” “Eh bien,” wrote Elie, “si célèbre, ma grand-mère Chafica !”

Le vœu. Un membre de sa famille était très malade, et Chafika fit un vœu : si le malade se tirait de ce mauvais pas, elle irait jusqu’à la synagogue les pieds nus — signe de repentir. Le patient rétabli, elle arriva angoissée au bureau d’Albert : comment accomplir sa promesse dans les rues de la ville ? Albert proposa une idée géniale : « Tu viendras à pied jusqu’à la synagogue. À la grille d’entrée tu enlèveras tes chaussures, et tu entreras pieds nus allumer une bougie. » Chafika était ravie de cette solution, qu’elle s’empressa de mettre à exécution.

Gaby Elia — « Le vœu de tante Chafika »

Le mariage. Dans notre communauté, la moitié seulement avait des papiers officiels — les arrivés trop tard, après 1948, étaient « apatrides » : un simple laissez-passer, le droit de quitter le pays sans pouvoir y retourner. Chafika, originaire de Damas, était coincée au Liban. Albert lui proposa d’épouser le très vieux Moussa Srour, qui était Libanais — un mariage blanc des deux côtés. Le jour de la cérémonie elle s’habille avec son élégance coutumière, écoute le rabbin avec bonne humeur — vive la mariée ! — puis regarde son nouvel époux de ses yeux pétillants : « Chou Moussa, ma fi boseh ? » Lui, le tarbouche sur la tête, aurait voulu être à dix mille lieues de là ! Par la suite, Albert a répété cette parfaite charade à plusieurs reprises : les femmes étaient bien contentes, et Moussa avait une rente viagère. L’amour-propre était sauvé.

Gaby Elia — « Le mariage de tante Chafika »

Le jugement. À la mort de leur père, deux frères se partagèrent l’héritage à parts égales — sauf le beau lustre en cristal, qu’ils convoitaient tous les deux. Le Beit Din trancha : les frères feraient don du candelabre à la synagogue Maghen Abraham. Ainsi chacun pourrait continuer à l’admirer, et toute la congrégation profiterait de sa lumière. Un jugement vraiment digne du roi Salomon.

Gaby Elia — « Le jugement »

And the group filled in the woman herself: she smoked the narguilé like Kamel Bazbaz and drank arak — “elle aimait vivre fort” (Elie B). “Elle avait les yeux violets d’Elizabeth Taylor” (Dani). “Je m’asseyais souvent avec elle au St Georges — elle commandait une bière. Un jour mon fils, deux ans, lui a chipé la bière : elle a rigolé” (Dani). “Tout le monde l’admirait — mon feu frère quittait son travail et venait à la maison pour la voir. Toujours très bien habillée, et ses yeux brillaient malicieusement” (Rina). “Puissante — je la craignais” (Elie B). “Beautiful and powerful Chafica” (Elie).

III

1948 — A Civilized Eviction

And then in 1948, after years of Arab-Jewish tensions, the big cataclysm happened in the Middle East: the creation of the state of Israel and the dispossession of 700,000 Palestinians who became refugees. In most Arab countries Jews were fired from public jobs. They were considered to be potential traitors.

Elie

Henri was a Directeur at the Banque de Syrie et du Liban — the Syrian central bank — around 1945. He was told: « Look, here we are the central bank and you see everything, all our payments. Every time we buy Hotchkiss machine guns from France, you see the payment. So we cannot keep you — we are transferring you to our branch in Beirut. »

That is how in 1950, at age 5, we moved from Damascus, Syria to Beirut, Lebanon.

Elie

Henri’s siblings scattered: Nathan to Egypt; Shafiq and Olga smuggled by Lebanese fishermen into Israel. “Happy we were out of Syria then.”

When we left Syria we lost our Syrian nationality. In Lebanon we had a permis de séjour, renewable yearly — but we were stateless, had no passports and could not travel. After many years we asked the Lebanese why we could not have nationality and a passport. They said: we don't give one to the Palestinians (300,000) — why should we give one to you?

Elie

One day Yvonne, looking at some papers in the drawer of our Damascus wooden sculpted buffet, found one Iranian passport torn in two. Henri took it to the Iranian Embassy and they said it was genuine — under the name Krayem Kermanchahi. So we became Iranians, with not one word of Farsi between us.

Elie

Then a few hundred of us made a donation to build a hospital for the Iranian Red Crescent, and the Shah signed a fermân and gave us the Iranian passport honoris causa. I used that passport until 1979.

Elie Bazbaz
IV

Damascus to Mexico — Ruben’s Letters

A new voice joined the book this week: Ruben Kreim of New York — grandson of Ruben, the brother of Khodr Krayem; raised in Mexico, Harvard Law. And with him his son Leon Kreim, 35, writing from Jerusalem. As Elie put it: “Khodr begat Henri. Henri begat Elie. Elie begat Michael-Henri. Michael-Henri begat Malakai.”

My father, Leon, was a very thoughtful, gentle man — but very quiet about his past. He was born in Damascus in 1913 (he always said it was « Damas, Iran », with a bit of a twinkle in his eye). The family moved to Egypt when he was very young; his memories were of Cairo and Alexandria. He left in the early 1930s, after his father Ruben died. I know he was in Kobe, in Mumbai and in Shanghai; in Tokyo soon after the war ended; in New York for some years, where he put together some wealth and then lost all of it in a bad investment — so he was pretty much back to square one when he started his trading life in Mexico. All of this is terribly vague and uncertain. I so wish now that I knew more.

Ruben Kreim

Nathan taught himself Spanish by doing an interlinear translation of his favourite French classics — by the time I was a little boy, he and I spoke in Spanish. He never married, but was quite the ladies' man. He died in a car accident in 1964 or 1965, which broke my father's heart. I don't think Leon ever quite recovered from that loss.

Ruben Kreim

When Nathan died, Leon and his sister Adelle decided that their mother Farida could not be told. They cooked up a story that Nathan was happily living in Mexico but could not leave because of visa issues. Thereafter, until Farida died in 1978 — that is, for about fifteen years — my father maintained a weekly correspondence with Farida, every letter beginning « chère maman » and signed « Natan », wherein Leon constructed an entire life story for his brother. Farida would write back every week, telling « Natan » how desperately she wished she could see him one more time. This correspondence, I fear, was lost when Farida died. I so wish I could have gotten my hands on it.

Ruben Kreim

Angus has set the group a challenge — his own family tree is a scroll four metres long — and Elie points to where the answers now live: the “very, very cohesive” Syrian Jewish community of Brooklyn, ninety thousand strong. Cohesive, as he says, from Damascus to Brooklyn.

Le dernier vendredi du Shabbat, mon père me fait la bénédiction habituelle — « Yebarekha hachem ve-yishmereka », que Dieu te bénisse et te protège — et il s'est mis à pleurer d'émotion. Elie Bazbaz at the piano — one of “les deux chansons hit de cet été-là”, summer 1962, the last summer.

Prague, août 1968. J'y étais, et j'ai vécu leur drame. Au lieu de m'enfuir comme tous les hommes d'affaires, je suis resté, solidaire. Ce soir-là je jouais au piano avec l'orchestre du bar de l'hôtel quand le concierge est venu nous dire de monter — on écoutait le bruit des avions au-dessus de Prague, qui parachutaient les troupes du pacte de Varsovie. During China's Cultural Revolution in the sixties, we were a few businessmen « invited » to China. The way in was Kowloon: crossing the bridge by foot with our luggage, arriving to loudspeakers shouting L'Internationale. The Chinese put me in a lounge with an Iranian — Ali Hijazi. He was stunned I couldn't say one word of Iranian and wanted to call the Red Guards to say I was a spy. I succeeded to quiet him… we became best friends for many years.

Elie Bazbaz

V

Beirut — “not Moslem, not Christian, and certainly not Jewish”

Henri's main issue with Beirut was the « Rtoube » — the humidity. Damascus was inland, surrounded by deserts, very dry. His friends had sternly warned him about the life-threatening rtoube of Beirut. So the decision on where to live was based on the rtoube — and also on Henri's determination to live as far away from the Jews as possible, that is not in Wadi Abou Jamil, the Jewish quarter. So he chose a kind of universal neighborhood near the national museum and the pine-wooded racecourse: not Moslem, not Christian, and certainly not Jewish. In fact, not a Jew for miles.

Elie

On the first floor lived the Chidiacs, a Christian Maronite extended family from Zahle. Raymond Chidiac one day showed me his very powerful hunting gun, a WINCHESTER — in fact a weapon. He really kept it to use one day in defence against Moslems: he knew in his gut that day would come.

Elie

On the top floor lived the Daibes, who ran a posh grocery up the road. I used to have kissing sessions with Alain's sister in the staircase — constantly scared the family would come down the stairs.

Elie

Zilzal — 16 March 1956

Today's topic: the 1956 earthquake — how did everyone live it? Me: it was Friday Shabbat and we were preparing to sleep when my bed started shaking, walls trembling. We all packed into father's Peugeot and — wrongly — drove along the seaside (tsunami unknown there). Then father moved us into my grandparents' house with the thick walls. We stayed ten days, and I became friends with my late uncle Fred Anzarouth — highly educated: he introduced me to the Ninth Symphony, and to Wagner's Ride of the Valkyries, que je prononçais « Vache-qui-rit ». I was happy not to go to school.

Elie Bazbaz

Yes, I remember the earthquake — I shared a room with my sister Norma. ZILZAL!! We went down to the street with all the neighbours. Norma recalls carrying my four-year-old sister Simone in her arms, down to the street with our ground-floor neighbours, the Chidiacs. No Ninth Symphony for us.

Elie — « and we slept in the car » (Simone)

Gina was not yet born — but her grandfather, very ill, died that night.

La douceur de vivre

La plupart des membres de notre communauté étaient bon enfants, heureux de leur sort — ils étaient satisfaits et se la coulaient douce, comme mon père et ma mère. Une jolie routine : boulot qui va, femme heureuse avec toutes ses sœurs chéries et des amis d'enfance… Déjeuner journalier à la maison, petite sieste, bureau de nouveau, et petits jeux de cartes ou partie de tric trac au club avant de rentrer chez soi. Des sorties et soirées mondaines entre amis où ça 🎵 chantait (Bint el Shalabiah et d'autres) et dansait. Yvonne et Joseph étaient spécialistes et partenaires de Charleston — appris à Damas. Haha 😝 Que de beaux souvenirs.

Muriel Salem

Très beau texte, Muriel, de notre vie quotidienne. Oui — Yvonne et Henri étaient champions du Charleston, je l'avais oublié. Et tu as oublié le Club, où mes parents et Chafica allaient très souvent.

Elie

Je n'ai jamais été plus juive libanaise que lorsque je me suis installée à Londres. Aujourd'hui je suis la dernière génération qui a connu le Liban. Je passe le bâton en préparant des sambousek, kebbé, hamod, balouza, maamoul, katayef — en racontant des histoires qui fascinent mes petits-enfants nés au 21ème siècle. Nous avons fait du chemin… mais nous n'avons rien oublié. Ni le bon, ni le mauvais, ni même l'indifférent. Hein, Elie ? Il suffit d'écouter 4 mots et 4 notes de Bint il Shalabieh — que mon père chantait si bien — pour y être. Je fête mes 70 ans le 22 août : je me permets enfin une touche de nostalgie.

Muriel Salem

Ah, le Labné de Chtaura en rentrant du festival de Baalbek… la semsmiyé à l'entrée des restaurants de Zahlé sur la Berdaouni — maintenant j'en ai le goût en bouche. Ah, le chant de Oum Kulthum dans la nuit super-étoilée de Baalbek…

VI

The Street

In August a new correspondent, Joe Dana, began sending Beirut in bursts — and the group answered him corner by corner: the tram, the beach clubs, the vendors, the characters. A city “perdue à 14 ans et jamais retrouvée.”

Beyrouth. Enfance inoubliable — inconscience et douceur de vivre. Rue Georges Picot et zaroub Saltoun, du nom de mon arrière-grand-père. Les familles Arazi, Abadi, Barzellai, Allouan… Hneinei qui vendait les cocas, boisson inestimable pour nous, fauchés. Le groupe inoubliable de mes copains — Jouky, Bibi, Cilo, Charlot, André, Moussy… Henriette, Flora, Rina… Les surprises-parties qui s'organisaient en quelques minutes et nous permettaient de nous serrer et de nous embrasser en dansant — que des slows.

Joe Dana

Beyrouth c'est aussi le tramway. Monter et descendre du tramway en marche — une activité normale qui ne représentait aucun risque pour notre jeunesse inconsciente. « Nazel hal mohattah » pour ne pas payer le billet quand le contrôleur arrivait.

Joe Dana — « same with me, Mohattah Renault 😅 » (Elie Bazbaz)

There is the famous Lebanese joke. Second wagon of the tram, the very back, terminus at Basta, Sunday August 15th. The percepteur steps on a man's foot. The man jumps: « Don't you know who I am?! » The percepteur replies: « It is the middle of the summer, at the terminus, at the back of the 5-piastre wagon. Anyone who is anyone is in the cool mountains. And who on earth can YOU be??! » — « Ya kaza kaza! »

Elie

The Lycée, Antoura & Cambridge

Le Lycée Français was really and truly one Mission Laïque Française. In a country deeply divided by community — Christians, Moslem Sunnis and Shias, Druzes and a few Jews — the Lycée received all communities.
